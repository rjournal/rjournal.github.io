\title{VizTest: Optimal Confidence Intervals for Visual Testing}


\author{by William Poirier and David A. Armstrong}

\maketitle

\abstract{%
When presented with several confidence intervals plotted together, readers have a natural inclination to try to compare the bounds visually to test for the statistical significance of their differences. Comparing confidence bounds this way to perform a pairwise significance test yields erroneous results. The VizTest R package provides researchers with optimal confidence intervals for visual representation of pairwise tests. It is natively compatible with basic regression model outputs and includes a function that creates usable objects from other outputs, such as: 1) multilevel regression; 2) predicted probabilities; and 3) descriptive quantities. Pairwise tests are performed natively via normal theory or simulations, but users can choose to provide the results of alternative procedures directly. Multiplicity adjustments are natively implemented. We provide thorough examples of these different applications to facilitate the integration of the package into the users' workflows.
}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Routinely, researchers convey the findings of their research through the visual display of estimates and confidence intervals \citep{few12b, kastellec07}. When readers engage these displays, they may naturally wonder whether estimates depicted in the visualization are statistically different from each other. Unfortunately, comparing the (non-)overlap of \(95\%\) confidence intervals does not equate to a test of difference at the \(5\%\) level. This is a problem that has been well understood for nearly a century. Both \citet{dice_1936} and \citet{simpson1939} recognized that \((1-\alpha)\times 100\%\) confidence intervals did not naturally equate to tests at the \(\alpha\) level. Their scholarship tried to identify rules of thumb for identifying differences that were either significant or not depending on the relative size of the standard errors of the estimates. \citet{browne79} developed the same type of rules for other displays (e.g., \(\pm 1SE\) bars) and provided more formalization around the problem of differing sampling variances. \citet{tukey1991} recognized that under the relatively restrictive assumption of equal standard errors and uncorrelated estimates \(84\%\) confidence intervals will overlap roughly \(95\%\) of the time under the null hypothesis. \citet{godlstein_healey_95} proposed a more general approach that would identify the appropriate confidence level for tests at any level under the same assumptions adopted by Tukey. \citet{payton_2003} extends this idea to the non-normal context. Papers describing methods for solving this problem have been cited over 7500 times across the medical, natural and social sciences.

The scholarship until 2025 had focused on identifying the appropriate inferential confidence level for \emph{a pair} of intervals. The idea was to identify the confidence level \((\gamma)\) such that confidence intervals for estimates with the same standard errors as the ones under investigation would overlap under the null hypothesis with probability \(\alpha\). Tukey's \(84\%\) confidence interval is such an example for estimates with standard errors of roughly equal size. Displays of estimates often have several intervals to be evaluated. It is unlikely that any single level \(\gamma\) will be optimal for all pairs of intervals. If it is not, then different tests will be performed with different type I error rates. If the only way to produce valid visual tests is that the confidence intervals overlap with the desired type I error rate under the null hypothesis, there is no general solution.

In \citet{armstrong_2025}, we propose a novel solution to this problem that identifies the confidence level(s) that best represent the results of \emph{all} pairwise tests between estimates. This paper describes the \CRANpkg{VizTest} package that implements this procedure in R. The package is natively compatible with any object that has \texttt{coef} and \texttt{vcov} methods and whose pairwise tests are approximately accurate under the assumption of normality. This includes most regression model outputs. In other cases, there is a function that creates an object that is compatible with the package's main function from predicted probabilities, descriptive statistics and simulation output, to name a few.

As motivation, consider the following simple regression model using the built-in \texttt{chickwts} dataset. We regress weight of the chicks on the type of feed they consumed. We plot the predicted weights for each feed type with \(95\%\) confidence intervals in Figure \ref{fig:chickwts-95ci}. The confidence intervals do not convey information that is useful for pairwise testing just based on the (non-)overlaps in the confidence intervals. Of course, intervals that do not overlap indicate estimates that are statistically different from each other. However, the converse is not true.

Our solution involves searching for confidence levels such that overlapping confidence intervals indicate estimates that are not statistically different from each other and non-overlapping intervals indicate estimates that are statistically different from each other. We describe the algorithm below and then describe the \CRANpkg{VizTest} package that implements it. We then provide several use cases that demonstrate how to use the package in a variety of analytical situations. We compare our solution with alternative visualizations (compact letter displays, significance brackets, heatmaps, and comparison arrows) in the supplementary material, highlighting advantages and drawbacks for each.

\section{Searching for inferential confidence intervals}\label{searching-for-inferential-confidence-intervals}

All previous solutions, up to and including the most recent intervention in this literature \citep{radean2023} use the probability of overlap in the intervals to do the test. That is, the visualization and the test are inextricably linked. We propose a solution that estimates pairwise tests of all estimates and then performs a grid search over a set of predefined, reasonable values to find a confidence level \(\gamma\), such that (non-)overlaps in the \(\gamma\times 100\%\) confidence intervals correspond as closely as possible with the (in)significant differences. That is, we decouple the visualization from the testing. The algorithm we propose and describe in \citet{armstrong_2025} is as follows:

\textbf{1. Conduct all pairwise tests between estimates.} In the cases of estimates with a variance-covariance matrix, use normal theory to produce pairwise tests of the differences among the estimates. These tests can be adjusted for multiplicity or the user can supply a robust variance-covariance matrix if desired. In the case of simulation output, where the estimates are a number of draws \(\times\) number of parameters matrix, the tests are done by calculating a matrix with differences between the vector of draws for each pair of estimates. The probability that the larger estimate is larger than the smaller estimate is then calculated and deemed ``significant'' if that probability is greater than \(1-\alpha\). This procedure produces a vector, \(s_{ij}\) that indicates whether estimate \(b_i\) is significantly different from estimate \(b_j\) (in which case \(s_{ij} = 1\), or zero otherwise).

\textbf{2. Find the inferential confidence level(s).} With the results from all the pairwise tests in \(\boldsymbol s\), we search over a range of values of \(\gamma\) to find the \((1-\gamma)\) confidence interval(s)\footnote{We call \((1-\gamma)\) the inferential confidence level, the confidence level that best visually represents the underlying pairwise tests.} such that the intervals for pairs of estimates that are significantly different from each other do not overlap and pairs of estimates that are not significantly different from each other do overlap.

\textbf{3. Pick the inferential confidence level that is most useful.} If multiple \(\gamma\) are found to be equivalent in step 2, we should try to identify which is ``best.'' While we discuss some alternatives below, the idea is that it should be as easy as possible to do two things -- identify where intervals do not overlap for those estimates that are statistically different from each other and identify where intervals do overlap for those estimates that are not statistically different from each other.

If a \(\gamma\) level is identified where \textbf{all} the pairwise tests match the visual tests, then using that level in the corresponding visualization would solve the aforementioned issue -- readers would be able to easily identify which pairs of estimates are statistically distinguishable from each other and which are not. This is implemented in the \CRANpkg{VizTest} package described below along with several use cases that will allow researchers to deploy the algorithm described above in a wide array of real-world analytical situations.

\section{\texorpdfstring{The \CRANpkg{VizTest} package}{The  package}}\label{the-package}

The \CRANpkg{VizTest} package allows the user to find the optimal confidence level for visual testing in many different scenarios. The \texttt{viztest()} function is a generic that currently dispatches one of two methods -- the \texttt{default} method which performs a normal theory test for pairwise difference and the \texttt{vtsim} method which operates on realized samples from a distribution (e.g., a matrix of samples from a Bayesian posterior distribution). It currently accepts the following arguments:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \texttt{obj}: the object that contains the estimates.
\item
  \texttt{test\_level}: the type 1 error rate (\(\alpha\)) for the pairwise tests (default at \(0.05\)).
\item
  \texttt{range\_levels}: the range of \(\gamma\) to try (default from \(0.25\) to \(0.99\)).
\item
  \texttt{level\_increment}: step size between values of \texttt{range\_levels} (default at \(0.01\)).
\item
  \texttt{adjust}: multiplicity adjustment (\texttt{holm}, \texttt{hochberg}, \texttt{hommel}, \texttt{bonferroni}, \texttt{BH}, \texttt{BY}, \texttt{fdr} or \texttt{none}) to use when computing the \(p\)-values for the pairwise tests. This is not implemented for the \texttt{sim} method. The default is to do no adjustment.\footnote{We do not adjust for multiple comparisons in the following examples, but we discuss its impact in a dedicated section of the supplementary material.}\\
\item
  \texttt{cifun}: the method used to calculate the confidence/credible interval for simulation results. Either ``quantile'' (default) or ``hdi'' for highest density region.
\item
  \texttt{include\_intercept}: logical whether to include the intercept in the pairwise tests (intercept excluded by default).
\item
  \texttt{include\_zero}: logical whether a univariate null hypothesis test should be included for each estimate (\texttt{TRUE} by default).
\item
  \texttt{sig\_diffs}: optional logical vector indicating, for each pair of estimates, whether or not there is a significant difference. This is \texttt{NULL} by default. There is a vignette to the package that shows how this can be used.
\item
  \texttt{tol}: tolerance level for evaluating the symmetry and positive-definiteness of the variance-covariance matrices (defaults to \texttt{1e-08}).
\item
  \texttt{...} Currently, no other arguments are passed down to other internal functions.
\end{itemize}

The package has dependencies on six other packages. First, \CRANpkg{dplyr} \citep{dplyr} and \CRANpkg{tidyr} \citep{tidyr} are used for different data wrangling tasks all throughout the implementation of the procedure. The \CRANpkg{ggplot2} package \citep{ggplot2} is used as the main driver of the plot method. \CRANpkg{HDInterval} \citep{HDInterval} is used for simulation results where the user specifies the use of highest density region (HDI) to calculate the confidence/credible interval as opposed to a conventional quantile interval. The \CRANpkg{multcomp} package \citep{multcomp} is used to compute adjusted confidence intervals for the original \texttt{test\_level} tests if they are requested. We import \texttt{emmobj()} from \CRANpkg{emmeans} \citep{emmeans} for the auxiliary function, \texttt{get\_letters()} that helps make compact letter displays. In the examples below, we also use \CRANpkg{broom} \citep{broom} and \CRANpkg{gt} \citep{gt} for formatting tabular output.

For the default method, normal theory tests are performed. Specifically, we use R's \texttt{combn()} function to identify all pairwise combinations of observation ids. For example, for a vector of estimates (call it \texttt{est} with five values) and a variance-covariance matrix (call it \texttt{v}, which is \(5\times5\)), we define \texttt{combs\ \textless{}-\ combn(length(est),2)} which would identify the ten different pairwise combinations. We reorder \texttt{est} so they are from largest to smallest and reorder the rows and columns of \texttt{v} accordingly, too. We then make a matrix that calculates the pairwise differences in the following way:

\begin{verbatim}
D <- matrix(0, nrow= length(est), ncol = ncol(combs))
D[cbind(combs[1,], 1:ncol(D))] <- -1
D[cbind(combs[2,], 1:ncol(D))] <- 1
\end{verbatim}

\noindent This produces a matrix, \texttt{D}, such that each column corresponds with a single pairwise test. We can then calculate the pairwise differences and their standard errors with:

\begin{verbatim}
diffs <- est %*% D
v_diffs <- t(D) %*% v %*% D
se_diffs <- sqrt(diag(v_diffs))
\end{verbatim}

\noindent Finally, we calculate \(z\)-statistics for all pairwise differences and their p-values using the standard normal CDF. If the object inherits the \texttt{"lm"} class and has a \texttt{df.residual} element, a t-test is used instead. The vector \(s\) above, is then defined as \(Pr(|z| > Z) <\) \texttt{test\_level} for the vector of \(z\)-statistics from the pairwise tests.

Next, we calculate the lower and upper confidence bounds for all test values of \(\gamma\). This produces a \texttt{length(est)}\(\times\)\texttt{diff(range\_levels)/level\_increment} matrix of lower (\texttt{L}) and upper (\texttt{U}) confidence bounds. Since the values of \texttt{est} are ordered from largest to smallest, if \(L_i > U_j\) then the confidence intervals for stimulus \(i\) and \(j\) do not overlap for any values of \(i < j \leq\) \texttt{length(est)}. Likewise, if \(L_i < U_j\), the two intervals do overlap. Thus, each column of the resulting matrix \texttt{L{[}combs{[}1,{]},{]}\ \textgreater{}\ U{[}combs{[}2,{]},\ {]}} contains a vector \(s^{*}\) that can be used to evaluate correspondence with \(s\).

For the simulation method, \texttt{est} is a \(\#\text{ draws} \times\) \texttt{length(est)} matrix where we calculate pairwise differences of the estimates using the \texttt{D} matrix defined above: \texttt{diffs\ \textless{}-\ est\ \%*\%\ D}. We then calculate the probability that each pairwise difference is greater than zero: \texttt{p\_diffs\ \textless{}-\ apply(diffs,\ 2,\ \textbackslash{}(x)mean(x\ \textgreater{}\ 0))}. Then, to treat credible values greater than zero and credible values smaller than zero the same, we calculate \texttt{p\_diffs\ \textless{}-\ ifelse(p\_diffs\ \textgreater{}\ .5,\ 1-p\_diffs,\ p\_diffs)}. We create the \(s\) vector as \texttt{p\_diffs\ \textless{}\ test\_level}. We use \texttt{cifun()} at level \(\gamma\) to calculate the lower and upper bounds of each estimate and proceed in the same fashion as above to define \(s^{*}\) and evaluate the correspondence between \(s\) and \(s^{*}\).

Regardless of the method, we identify the level(s) of \(\gamma\) that produce the greatest correspondence between \(s\) and \(s^{*}\). Below, we describe some of the function arguments in greater detail as well as some of the subsequent generic methods (i.e., \texttt{print()} and \texttt{plot()}) which are defined for objects returned by \texttt{viztest()}.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The \texttt{obj} argument}{The obj argument}}\label{the-obj-argument}

The \texttt{obj} argument can be of two different broad types. The default method of \texttt{viztest()} uses \texttt{coef()} and \texttt{vcov()} to extract the estimates and their variance-covariance matrix, respectively. Obviously, this will not work as intended for all analytical situations of interest. To that end, we provide a function \texttt{make\_vt\_data()} that can take a vector of estimates and either a vector of standard errors (in the case of independent estimates) or a variance-covariance matrix in the case of non-independent samples. If a vector of standard errors is provided, a variance-covariance matrix will be produced by multiplying the vector of standard errors by the appropriately sized identity matrix. The final argument of \texttt{make\_vt\_data()} is an argument identifying the type of input data. If \texttt{type\ =\ "est\_var"} the function assumes that \texttt{estimates} contains point estimates and \texttt{variances} either a vector of standard errors or a variance-covariance matrix. In this case, the function returns an object of class \texttt{vtcustom}. We have defined \texttt{coef()} and \texttt{vcov()} methods for \texttt{.vtcustom} objects so that \texttt{viztest()} will work as expected in this case. We anticipate two primary use-cases for this function, though others will likely arise, too.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Users may want to replace the default variance-covariance matrix with a robust alternative. In that case, calling \texttt{make\_vt\_data()} on the original estimates and the robust variance-covariance matrix will allow the pairwise tests to be calculated with the robust variance-covariance matrix.\\
\item
  It is possible that the user would want to make a plot with a collection of descriptive statistics that do not derive from a single object. In that case, assuming they are independent, the vector of estimates and standard errors could be provided to \texttt{make\_vt\_data()} to evaluate all statistics at the same time.
\end{enumerate}

For simulation results, users can also use the \texttt{make\_vt\_data()} function. In this case, the \texttt{estimates} argument is the draws\(\times\)estimates matrix of simulated values. The \texttt{variances} argument remains \texttt{NULL} and \texttt{type} is set to \texttt{"sim"}. This produces an object of class \texttt{.vtsim}, that can be passed to \texttt{viztest}. Note, that if \texttt{type\ =\ "sim"}, \texttt{variances} will be disregarded even if they are specified.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The \texttt{sig\_diffs} argument}{The sig\_diffs argument}}\label{the-sig_diffs-argument}

We provide the option for the user to input the vector \(s\) directly with the \texttt{sig\_diffs} argument. The specification of this argument is a bit complex. While it is just a logical vector, care must be taken to ensure that the order of the values is appropriate. The complexity here comes from the fact that, internally, the estimates and variance-covariance matrix are rearranged in decreasing order of the estimates. To aid in the process, we provide a function \texttt{make\_diff\_template()} that takes three arguments -- \texttt{estimates}, the same estimated values that will be passed to \texttt{viztest()}, \texttt{include\_zero} and \texttt{include\_intercept}, which should be set to the same values as they are in the \texttt{viztest()} function (for convenience, they have the same default values in both places). The function simply reorders the estimates and identifies the pairwise combinations the same way that \texttt{viztest()} does. It returns a data frame that contains the names (taken from \texttt{names(estimates)}) of the estimates for the larger and smaller estimate in each pair. The resulting data frame can be merged with estimates or any other values with the same names as the estimates that can help identify the significance or credibility of pairwise differences. If this is provided, it overrides the calculation of the pairwise tests in \texttt{viztest()}. There is a vignette that accompanies the R package that discusses how to use \texttt{sig\_diffs} and \texttt{make\_diff\_template()}.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\texttt{viztest()} returned values}{viztest() returned values}}\label{viztest-returned-values}

The \texttt{viztest()} function returns a list containing 9 elements:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \texttt{tab}: a table returning the results of the tests for all values of \(\gamma\). This table is composed of 4 columns:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \texttt{level}: the tested \(\gamma\)s. The default behaviour is to perform a two-tailed test at this \(\gamma\) level. Use \(2\times\gamma\) to perform a one-tailed test.
  \item
    \texttt{psame}: the percentage of agreement between the pairwise test and the visualization test (\(s_{ij}\) and \(s_{ij}^*\)) for corresponding levels of \(\gamma\) (higher is better).
  \item
    \texttt{pdiff}: the percentage of statistically significant differences in pairwise tests (\(s_{ij}\)).
  \item
    \texttt{easy}: the easiness measure for each \(\gamma\) (higher is better).
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \texttt{pw\_test}: \(s_{ij}\), a Boolean vector of significance of pairwise tests.
\item
  \texttt{ci\_tests}: \(s_{ij}^*\), a Boolean vector of significance of visual CI tests.
\item
  \texttt{combs}: the matrix describing all the tested pairs.
\item
  \texttt{param\_names}: the vector of covariates' names in descending order.
\item
  \texttt{L}: the matrix of lower bounds for each covariate (rows) across all tested \(\gamma\) (columns).
\item
  \texttt{U}: the matrix of upper bounds for each covariate (rows) across all tested \(\gamma\) (columns).
\item
  \texttt{est}: a sub-list containing the variable's names (\texttt{vbl}), the original estimates (\texttt{est}), their standard errors (\texttt{se}), and their lower (\texttt{lwr\_add}) and upper (\texttt{upr\_add}) confidence bounds at the original \texttt{test\_level}.
\item
  \texttt{call}: an object storing the original call to the function.
\end{itemize}

The object also has two additional defined attributes: \texttt{"adjusted"}, giving the method used for multiplicity adjustment or \texttt{"none"} if none was used and \texttt{"test\_level"}, giving the type I error rate used for the tests.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Choosing among different \(\gamma\) levels}{Choosing among different \textbackslash gamma levels}}\label{choosing-among-different-gamma-levels}

In the event that there are multiple values of \(\gamma\) that produce identical correspondence between \(s\) and \(s^{*}\), how should we choose among the possibilities? In essence every level ``works,'' but some will make the perceptual tasks required to decode the visualization easier than others. In general, this type of display -- inferential confidence intervals plotted along the same axis -- is ideal for being able to discriminate even small distances \citep{cleveland84}. \citet{Lu2022} discuss how people perceive ``just noticeable differences'' (JNDs) in different kinds of displays -- the bar chart being the one most applicable to our situation. They find that the smallest distinguishable distance between two bars grows as the distance between the bars increases. That is, for vertical bars, the farther they are apart on the horizontal axis, the larger the height difference has to be to be visually distinguishable. Given that stimuli have potentially different variances and consequently different size confidence intervals, it is impossible to always place intervals that will be difficult to distinguish from one another in close proximity. This means that we should try to choose \(\gamma\) such that it makes the perceptual task as easy as possible. We do two things to try to make this as easy as possible.

First, we identify the level that will produce plots that require the least cognitive effort to decode. That is, we identify a level that, as much as possible, makes intervals where the relevant overlaps (or non-overlaps) are larger than just noticeable.

To motivate the discussion, we return to the chick weights example above. Here, we are interested in the predictions from the model. We can make those predictions with the \CRANpkg{marginaleffects} package \citep{marginaleffects}. After generating the predictions, we can run \texttt{viztest()} on the resulting object. The output below shows that the tests for all pairs of estimates are appropriately captured by the (non-)overlaps of confidence intervals between 68\% and 85\%.

\begin{verbatim}
#> 
#> Correspondents of PW Tests with CI Tests
#>   level psame pdiff        easy  method
#> 1  0.68     1   0.8 -13.9378307  Lowest
#> 2  0.76     1   0.8  -2.4617453  Middle
#> 3  0.85     1   0.8 -14.3551587 Highest
#> 4  0.78     1   0.8  -0.8146977 Easiest
#> 
#> All  15  tests properly represented for by CI overlaps.
\end{verbatim}

Figure \ref{fig:chick-wts-mult} plots the boundary intervals (68\% and 85\%) as well as the midpoint (76\%) and what we term the ``easiest'' level (78\%). How easy the display is to engage and how big the differences are that readers will need to distinguish depends on two pairs of estimates in every result. The top row in the figure (labelled ``Test 1'') shows the first of these tests -- the test for the pair of estimates that are significantly different from each other, but whose confidence intervals are closest to overlapping among all significantly different pairs. In our example below, these two estimates are casein and meatmeal. The 68\% interval makes it clearest that there is a difference between these two estimates because the distance between the upper bound for meatmeal and the lower bound for casein is largest. At the other end, using the 85\% level it is very difficult to see whether the intervals just overlap or just do not overlap. This difference between the meatmeal upper bound and casein lower bound likely is not noticeable. We want to choose a level that makes significant differences easy to identify. The bottom row of the figure presents the second test. This one is for the two estimates that are not statistically different from each other, but whose confidence intervals are closest to not overlapping among all pairs of estimates that are not statistically different from each other. In our example, these are for meatmeal and soybean. Here, the 85\% interval makes it clearest that the two confidence intervals for meatmeal and soybean overlap. Just like the 85\% interval did for the first test, the 68\% interval makes it nearly impossible to identify whether the intervals for soybean and meatmeal overlap or not. As you can see, picking values at the ends of the range will tend to highlight either the significant differences or the insignificant differences at the expense of the other. The 76\% interval makes both tests relatively clear as a value in the middle of the range will tend to do. However, we propose a measure that essentially tries to equalize the size of the overlap for Test 2 and the non-overlap (e.g., the distance between the meatmeal upper bound and the casein lower bound) for Test 1. This is how we define ``cognitive easiness.'' This is the value that makes the differences as large as possible (hopefully beyond the JND boundary) for the two tests that are most prone to inferential errors. Here, the 78\% interval is the one that does that.

The other potential solution is to draw reference lines for ambiguous pairs of estimates -- those that are difficult to distinguish. The \texttt{plot()} method for \texttt{viztest} objects permits this kind of annotation. \subsection{\texorpdfstring{Objects where \texttt{coef()} and \texttt{vcov()} work}{Objects where coef() and vcov() work}}\label{objects-where-coef-and-vcov-work}

For this example, we use the \texttt{gpa1} dataset from the \CRANpkg{wooldridge} package. We create a regression model using \texttt{lm}. Models created from \texttt{lm} and \texttt{glm} can directly be used in \texttt{viztest} as we do here. In fact, any object that has a defined \texttt{coef()} method that returns estimates and a \texttt{vcov()} method that returns the variance-covariance matrix of the estimates will work this way. We also make explicit the default values of the \texttt{test\_level}, \texttt{range\_levels}, and \texttt{level\_increment}. The \texttt{print()} method for \texttt{viztest} objects will print a few different pieces of information. First, it prints a table that identifies the smallest, middle, largest and easiest values of inferential confidence intervals that maximize the correspondence between the pairwise tests and the (non-)overlaps of the confidence intervals. In some cases, this might identify a single value or very small range. In other cases, it could identify quite a large range.

If the \texttt{missed\_tests} argument is \texttt{TRUE} (its default), then the printed results will either indicate that all tests have been accounted for or it will identify which tests are not accommodated by the inferential confidence intervals. The output below indicates that the optimal CI is at the \(80\%\) level and that this yields two errors. Which tests are not covered depends on the chosen level. The smallest, middle and easiest levels produce results where the coefficients for \texttt{alcohol} and \texttt{car} are not significantly different from zero (see the \texttt{Insig} designation in the \texttt{pw\_test} column), but their inferential CIs do not include zero (see the \texttt{No} designation in the \texttt{ci\_olap} column). The highest value in the range (94\%) produces different errors. Here, the pairwise tests between \texttt{PC} and \texttt{male} as well as the test between \texttt{alcohol} and \texttt{car} are significant, but the 94\% intervals still overlap.\footnote{Setting \texttt{include\_zero=FALSE} shrinks the acceptable range from {[}73\%, 94\%{]} to {[}73\%, 84\%{]} because the univariate tests would not be considered, so the values in the second interval would account for all the pairwise tests.} This means that no one inferential CI can visually represent all pairwise test \emph{and} all univariate tests against zero. That said, all the pairwise difference tests are accommodated by the 80\% interval.

There are two ways this could be handled. First, we could simply note in the figure caption the two tests that are not appropriately captured by the inferential confidence intervals. Alternatively, if the univariate tests against zero are important to convey, the standard (1-\texttt{test\_level}) confidence intervals will appropriately capture those. Those intervals could be plotted as well, though the figure caption should still indicate the discrepancy. The \texttt{plot()} method for these objects overlays the inferential and standard confidence intervals by default.

The \texttt{plot} method for the object then yields panel (a) of Figure \ref{fig:reg-obj-plot}. By default, it plots the estimates and their optimal CI using the \texttt{geom\_point()} and \texttt{geom\_linerange()} function from \CRANpkg{ggplot2}, as well as the (1-\texttt{test\_level}) confidence interval. The original \texttt{test\_level} can be removed from the plot with \texttt{add\_test\_level=FALSE}. In addition to the \texttt{viztest} object, the method takes several arguments:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \texttt{add\_test\_level}: logical indicating whether the (1-\texttt{test\_level}) confidence intervals should also be plotted (if returned from the \texttt{viztest()} function). Default is \texttt{TRUE}.
\item
  \texttt{ref\_lines}: one of \texttt{all}, \texttt{ambiguous}, \texttt{none}, or a vector of coefficient names (they must match the names included in the \texttt{viztest} object). \texttt{all} will plot vertical dotted lines along the upper bound of the lowest coefficient to the most distant coefficient with overlapping confidence intervals. \texttt{ambiguous} will draw these lines but only for the overlapping bounds that are near to each other. \texttt{none}, the default value, won't draw reference lines. There is also the option of manually feeding the argument a vector of coefficient names for which the user wants reference lines drawn.
\item
  \texttt{viz\_diff\_thresh}: threshold value for identifying ambiguity that will be used if \texttt{ref\_lines\ =\ "ambiguous"}. Default at \(0.02\).
\item
  \texttt{make\_plot}: a Boolean value indicating whether to directly plot the results of the object (\texttt{TRUE}, the default) or to return a \texttt{dataframe} that can be used to make a custom visualization.
\item
  \texttt{level}: allows users to choose the CI level they want, either through a numerical value or one of the following: \texttt{ce} for cognitively easiest, \texttt{max} for highest, \texttt{min} for smallest, and \texttt{median} for the middle value. The default is to return the cognitively easiest.
\item
  \texttt{trans}: a function that transforms the coefficients and their CI. Default is the identity function \texttt{I}, i.e.~no transformation. Note, non-linear transformations do not trigger a recalculation of the test statistics, they simply result in a transformation of the estimate and the ends of the confidence bounds.
\item
  \texttt{est\_point\_args}: A list of arguments to be passed to \texttt{geom\_point()} that plots the point estimates. The default is \texttt{list(color="black",\ size=2)}.
\item
  \texttt{opt\_ci\_args}: A list of arguments to be passed to \texttt{geom\_linerange()} to plot the optimal visual testing intervals. The default is a blank list.
\item
  \texttt{test\_ci\_args}: A list of arguments to be passed to \texttt{geom\_linerange()} to plot the (1-test level) confidence intervals. The default is a blank list.
\item
  \texttt{ref\_line\_args}: A list of arguments to be passed to \texttt{geom\_segment()} to plot the reference lines. The default is \texttt{list(color="gray75",\ linetype=3)}
\item
  \texttt{scale\_linewidth\_args}: A list of arguments to be passed to \texttt{scale\_linewidth\_manual()} to change the thickness of the confidence intervals. The default is \texttt{list(values=c(3.5,\ .5))}.
\item
  \texttt{scale\_color\_args}: A list of arguments to be passed to \texttt{scale\_color\_manual()} to change the default colour of the different confidence intervals when \texttt{add\_test\_level=TRUE}. The default is \texttt{list(values\ =\ c("gray75",\ "black"))}.
\item
  \texttt{overall\_theme}: A theme function that will be passed to the \texttt{ggplot} call before \texttt{theme()}. Default is \texttt{theme\_bw}.
\item
  \texttt{theme\_arg}: A list of arguments to be passed to \texttt{theme()} to modify the theme of the plot. The default is \texttt{list(legend.position="top")}.
\item
  \texttt{remove\_caption}: A caption is printed by default that alerts the user to the adjustment of the confidence intervals for visual testing. If \texttt{remove\_caption=TRUE}, the caption will be removed from the plot.
\end{itemize}

There are a few different ways that we could account for tests not accommodated by the inferential confidence intervals. We could simply discuss them in the figure's note. In this case, we would simply add a note to the effect: ``Even though the \texttt{alcohol} inferential confidence interval does not include zero, it is not statistically different from zero in a proper pairwise test.'' In this particular case (or any case where tests against zero are not captured), the \(95\%\) confidence intervals will capture those tests (since they are univariate tests against a point null hypothesis). Hence, by simply adding the \(95\%\) confidence intervals to the plot, we solve this specific case's missed tests. In situations where \(95\%\) confidence intervals aren't necessary and might obstruct a more complex visualization exercise, setting \texttt{add\_test\_level=FALSE} will remove them as shown in panel (b) of Figure \ref{fig:reg-obj-plot}.

\subsubsection{Replacing or adding to default confidence intervals}\label{replacing-or-adding-to-default-confidence-intervals}

In \citet{armstrong_2025}, we follow the convention in the literature and suggest replacing the default (e.g., \(95\%\)) confidence intervals with the ones that best accomplish the visual testing goal. This is the reasoning that many are using for presenting \(84\%\) intervals -- not because they are inherently interested in \(84\%\) confidence, but because they facilitate pairwise tests at the \(5\%\) level.

We recognize that for many, trying to publish a paper with, say, \(72\%\) confidence intervals and convincing reviewers that they are not doing something disingenuous will be a heavy lift. In these cases, there is no harm in also presenting the \(95\%\) intervals. The only real cost is increased cognitive load required to decode the graph owing to the two different sets of intervals. If they are well explained, they should provide sufficient information to both allay fears of reviewers and provide readers with the ability to perform any arbitrary set of pairwise tests. Hence, the present version of the package displays the original test's intervals by default.

\subsection{Multilevel regression objects}\label{multilevel-regression-objects}

For this example we use the World Values Survey dataset from \CRANpkg{carData} and produce a multilevel regression model using \texttt{lmer()} from the \CRANpkg{lme4} package.

\begin{verbatim}
#### 4.2 Multilevel regression objects ####
data(WVS, package = 'carData')

# Poverty variable as a scale from -1 to 1
NewWVS <- WVS %>%
  mutate(povertyNum = as.numeric(poverty) - 2)

# The model
model2 <- lmer(povertyNum ~ age + religion +
                 degree + gender + (1 | country), NewWVS)
\end{verbatim}

Here, the results have class \texttt{lmerMod} which is not readily usable by \texttt{viztest}. For \texttt{lmerMod} and \texttt{merMod} objects, \texttt{coef()} returns a matrix of coefficients with level-2 observations in the rows and variables in the columns. Thus, \texttt{coef()} is defined, but produces output that is not compatible with \texttt{viztest()}. To proceed, we need to create a custom object; we can do this with \texttt{make\_vt\_data()}. First, we extract the fixed effects from the model and assign them cleaner names for the subsequent plot. Second, we extract the variance-covariance matrix using \texttt{vcov} which in the case of \texttt{lmerMod} objects outputs an object of class \texttt{dpoMatrix}. The \texttt{make\_vt\_data()} function does a test of whether the input variances inherit the ``matrix'' class. This fails for \texttt{dpoMatrix} objects, so we recast the object as a \texttt{matrix} using \texttt{as.matrix}. Now that we have both a named vector of coefficients and its corresponding variance-covariance matrix, we create a compatible object fed to \texttt{viztest()}.

\begin{verbatim}
# Creating custom object
## Extracting fixed effect coefficients and naming them
named_coef_vec <- fixef(model2)
names(named_coef_vec) <- c("(Intercept)", "Age", "Religious", "Has a degree", "Male")

## Extracting vcov matrix
#### As matrix necessary to overwrite special object from lme4
vcov <- as.matrix(vcov(model2))

eff_vt <- make_vt_data(named_coef_vec, vcov)

# Parsing to viztest
viztestObj <- viztest(
  eff_vt,
  test_level = 0.05,
  range_levels = c(0.25, 0.99),
  level_increment = 0.01
)

# Print
print(viztestObj)
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Predicted probabilities}\label{predicted-probabilities}

For this example, we use the \texttt{TitanicSurvival} dataset from \CRANpkg{carData} to create predicted probabilities of survival based on a logistic regression model. We first create a variable corresponding to age categories and then run a model including an interaction between sex and this new categorical age variable. We use the \texttt{avg\_predictions()} function from \CRANpkg{marginaleffects} to produce predicted values for each pair of values of the interaction. One of the benefits of using the functions in \CRANpkg{marginaleffects} is that they have defined \texttt{coef()} and \texttt{vcov()} methods which make them compatible with \texttt{viztest()}. However, one thing to note is that by default, the standard errors are calculated with the delta method and the confidence intervals are normal theory intervals around the estimated values. For predicted probabilities close to zero or one, users may want to use a different method. We show both below.

\subsubsection{Normal theory intervals}\label{normal-theory-intervals}

Since the default behaviour for \texttt{avg\_predictions()} is to use normal theory intervals, we do not need to intervene in the function. First, we can manage the data, estimate the model and then calculate the average predicted probabilities.

\subsubsection{Using simulation method}\label{using-simulation-method}

The \CRANpkg{marginaleffects} functions have an inference engine that will treat the input models as Bayesian (assuming ignorance priors) and sample from the multivariate normal distribution centred at the coefficient estimates with a variance-covariance matrix equal to the analytical variance-covariance matrix from the model. We can invoke this with the \texttt{inferences()} function.\footnote{We direct the interested reader to \citet{arel-bundock_models_2026}, chapter 14, section 3 where the author of the \CRANpkg{marginaleffects} package discusses the procedure behind the quasi-Bayesian simulations produced by the \texttt{inferences()} function. We also provide advice on simulation length in the supplementary material.}

\begin{verbatim}
ap_sim <- avg_predictions(model3, variables = list(
  ageCat = levels(NewTitanicSurvival$ageCat),
  sex = levels(NewTitanicSurvival$sex)
)) %>%
  inferences(method = "simulation", R = 2500)
\end{verbatim}

We then pass that to \texttt{make\_vt\_data()} with the argument \texttt{type="sim"} and call \texttt{viztest()}. Inferential highest density intervals between \(84\%\) and \(95\%\) all account for the pairwise tests perfectly. The \(91\%\) HDIs are easiest to evaluate.

\begin{verbatim}
post <- t(attr(ap_sim, "posterior"))
colnames(post) <- paste0("b", 1:ncol(post))

vt_sim <- viztest(post_vt, cifun="hdi", include_zero=FALSE)
vt_sim

\subsection{Forest plot}\label{forest-plot}

Forest plots are more common in medical studies than in the social sciences. They are often used for visualizing the results of a meta-analysis, though they could be used for any set of coefficient estimates, too. While the many forest plotting functions in R are quite general, they tend not to anticipate our particular use case -- printing two confidence intervals for a single estimate. Functions like \texttt{forestplot()} from the \CRANpkg{forestplot} package \citep{forestplot} and \texttt{forest()} from the \CRANpkg{forestploter} package can be used in this case, but adding another confidence interval is idiosyncratic and hacky. It makes the endeavour not particularly user-friendly. To alleviate some of these problems, we have created a suite of \texttt{geom\_} functions that work with \CRANpkg{ggplot2} to make forest plots. We describe the functions here, but also detail them more fully in the package vignette on creating forest plots. For data, we use the \texttt{catheter} data \citep{veenstra1998antiseptic} from the \CRANpkg{rmeta} package \citep{rmeta}. We discuss the workflow for creating the plot below.

First, we need to load the data and we can use the \texttt{meta.MH()} function from the \CRANpkg{rmeta} package to estimate the Mantel-Haenszel log-odds ratios for each study along with its standard error.

\begin{verbatim}
library(rmeta)
data(catheter)

## Remove NA observations for the outcome variables
catheter <- catheter[complete.cases(catheter[, c("col.trt", "col.ctrl")]), ]

## Estimate the MH model for the data
cath <- meta.MH(n.trt,
                n.ctrl,
                col.trt,
                col.ctrl,
                names = Name,
                data = catheter)
cath
\end{verbatim}

\begin{verbatim}
#> Fixed effects ( Mantel-Haenszel ) Meta-Analysis

The \texttt{gg\_forest()} function uses several functions from \texttt{VizTest} that were designed to help build forest plots -- \texttt{geom\_forestpoint()}, \texttt{geom\_foreststripe()}, \texttt{geom\_foresttable()} and \texttt{scale\_x\_foresttable()}. These can be used to build the individual table and figure components of the forest plot for more customizable look. The \texttt{gg\_forest()} function is a wrapper that makes constructing the plots a bit easier. The \texttt{y} variable should be ordered in the way the values on the y-axis should appear. The \texttt{x} variable should be the estimate. The \texttt{xmin\_std} and \texttt{xmax\_std} variables are the standard confidence intervals and \texttt{xmin\_ici} and \texttt{xmax\_ici} are the inferential confidence intervals. The \texttt{size\_prop} variable is used to scale the point sizes. The \texttt{is\_summary} variable is used to indicate which rows are summary rows, so they can be plotted differently. The \texttt{use\_log\_scale} argument indicates whether the x-axis should be on the log scale. The \texttt{data\_cols} argument is a named vector indicating which variables in the data should be used for the table columns and what their labels should be. The \texttt{max\_size} argument indicates the maximum size of the points; this may take some trial and error to get just right. The \texttt{table\_header\_size} and \texttt{table\_text\_size} arguments indicate the size of the table header and text, respectively. The \texttt{col\_nudge} argument indicates how much to nudge the table columns to the left or right. This is particularly useful for left-aligned columns. In that case, the left edge of the text will appear aligned with the tick mark, but the header label will be centred on the tick mark. As such, left-aligned columns will often have to be moved to the left slightly. Finally, the \texttt{diamond\_aspect} and \texttt{diamond\_row\_frac} arguments indicate how to plot the summary diamond -- its aspect ratio and the maximum amount of the row it should take up, respectively. The object, of class \texttt{gg\_forest} contains two elements -- a table and figure, both ggplot objects. \section{Computational performance and efficiency}\label{computational-performance-and-efficiency}

There are a couple of aspects of computational performance and efficiency that bear consideration. First is, what is the computational load of \texttt{viztest()}? The answers are different depending on the context.

When using normal theory tests, the models need not be re-estimated, so the initial sample size and model complexity are of no real importance. The function simply uses the estimates and variance-covariance matrix to perform the relevant tests and make the relevant confidence intervals. What does matter to some degree is the number of estimates. For \(m\) estimates, there are \(\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\) pairs of estimates that require evaluation. We also imagine that our approach will fail in high dimensions. By failure we don't mean that it will not ``work,'' we mean that the display that would be generated would be too difficult to decode to be of much use. We don't know the exact number and that may depend on context, but 100 estimates seems like a reasonable first guess. If we think of this as the boundary size of inputs to our analysis, we can give some descriptive evidence about computation times. For reference, our computer is an Apple M2 Max with 96 GB of RAM using macOS Sequoia 15.6.1.

With 100 estimates the average time to run \texttt{viztest()} with the default grid without a multiplicity adjustment is a bit less than one second. There is considerable overhead when using \texttt{glht()} to calculate the confidence intervals with multiplicity adjustments, so the average run time increases to around seven seconds for the Holm adjustment and around 9 seconds for the Benjamini and Hochberg adjustment. So, even in these cases, computation time is not particularly problematic. This operation on 100 estimates used about 228 MB of RAM.

The simulation results are where the real concern lies. The reason is that the algorithm does not calculate the means and variance-covariance matrix from the simulation and use normal theory to do the tests, it uses the full simulation distribution. This means that both the number of estimates and the number of simulations matter. Figure \ref{fig:perfo} shows the memory usage and elapsed time under different simulation loads. For 100 estimates and 10,000 simulation draws, the \texttt{viztest()} on the default grid takes around 6 seconds and uses around 1.1GB of RAM. The time required by the algorithm and the memory usage scale about linearly with the number of simulation draws (time=25 seconds, RAM = 5.5GB for 50,000 draws, time=50 seconds, RAM = 10.9GB for 100,000 draws). We used \texttt{gc()} to calculate the maximum memory used before and after running the function and took the difference to get these figures. This memory usage does not include the overhead needed to store the simulation object itself. The RAM usage seems more problematic than the time -- the algorithm still completes in under a minute even with 100 estimates and 100,000 draws.

\begin{figure}
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{RJ-2026-059_files/figure-latex/perfo-1} \caption{\textbf{Computation Time and RAM Usage by Number of Estimates and Number of Draws for Simulation Studies}}\label{fig:perfo}
\end{figure}

If the simulation length is problematic, users could consider calculating the means and the variance-covariance matrix from the simulation and use the normal theory tests as an approximation. This will likely not be perfect, but there are many times where regression results or predictions could be transformed into something approaching normality. For example, predicted probabilities could be subjected to the log-odds transformation and then the means and variances calculated on that scale would be well suited to normal theory tests. The results could then be transformed back into the probability scale if desired.

Another aspect of computational efficiency worth considering is the resolution of the grid. The default grid has 75 values in increments of 0.01 between .25 and .99. This is likely sufficient for most applications. However, there may be reasons to consider either a finer or coarser grid. A coarser grid will decrease computation time, but may not find the optimal value that perfectly corresponds with the pairwise tests even if it exists. A finer grid will simply take longer to run, though it will be more likely to arrive at an optimal solution. Considering our World Values Survey Post-materialism example from above, we could see how this works out. We will use an unadjusted analysis and one with the Holm adjustment because they show slightly different things.

Figure \ref{fig:short-grid} shows the proportion of pairwise tests captured by visual testing intervals using a low-resolution grid (15 evaluation points). The left panel shows the unadjusted result and suggests zooming in between .8 and .9. The right panel shows the Holm adjustment and suggests extending the range to higher values and starting much closer to 1, say around .95. We could use this information to target those areas with a higher resolution. Figure \ref{fig:long-grid} shows the proportion of pairwise tests captured by the visual testing levels, but using a higher-resolution grid that is more targeted along the x-axis. The optimal values are clearly identifiable in this figure. This general idea could be used by others to maximize computational efficiency.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The examples above provide a lot of detail on how the package works and how users might discuss the findings in their own work. However, there are a few topics that may be of interest that were not covered above. We turn to some brief discussions of those below.

\subsection{What if it doesn't work}\label{what-if-it-doesnt-work}

Perhaps one of the most important issues is what happens when the function doesn't work. The \texttt{viztest()} function always produces an answer. However, as we showed, sometimes no level corresponds perfectly with the pairwise tests. How the user proceeds likely depends on their goals and how many (or maybe what proportion) of the tests are missed. If only a few tests are missed, users may decide to just discuss those discrepancies in the text or the note of the corresponding figure. If many tests are missed, perhaps another method would be preferred, like one of the heat maps. We do not think it makes sense to propose a strict cutoff above which users should use a different method. It really comes down to the ease of discussing the missed cases and the importance of presenting the intervals. If there are some estimates that are less important, users may consider dropping the less important estimates to see if that helps.

When there is no single level that works, breaking estimates into groups, if possible, could work. Having more than one group of estimates and allowing each group to have its own inferential confidence level could generate perfect correspondence within groups, but will necessarily leave across-group comparisons indeterminate. If there are natural groupings of estimates, this could be a useful solution. One example of this would be like our Iyengar and Westwood replication. In their article, there was a three-category treatment variable -- Democratic candidate most qualified, Republican candidate most qualified and both candidates equally qualified. Had the algorithm not produced a single perfect interval, we could have broken the estimates into treatment groups and then focused on within-treatment tests.

Another thing to consider is whether tests relative to zero are included. Including tests against zero (or any point null) imposes an extra set of constraints that may make finding an appropriate level more difficult. If the user also includes standard confidence intervals (e.g., 95\%), then those tests against a point null (including zero) can be accomplished by considering the overlap of the standard confidence intervals with the null hypothesis value. The inferential confidence intervals would then only have to capture the pairwise tests of estimates relative to each other.

Finally, one edge case where confidence intervals get strange is with data in categorical choice models that have perfect separation -- no variance on the dependent variable for some category of an independent variable \citep{Firth1993, Zorn2005}. In these cases, the coefficients tend toward the boundaries of the parameter space (e.g., very large positive or negative values). They generally have very large standard errors and confidence intervals that are extremely wide. Below is a simple example of what these models can look like:

\subsection{Stability of the results -- does sample size or variance matter?}\label{stability-of-the-results-does-sample-size-or-variance-matter}

One reviewer asked about the stability of these results (i.e., do sample size or variances matter)? The answer, we suppose, is ``probably.'' Since the input to the function is generally the output from a model (or other quantities derived from a model), the sample size and sampling variability will at least matter insofar as they matter for those estimates. We do not see a strong systematic relationship between sample size and the ability of the function to find an inferential confidence interval that corresponds with the pairwise tests. This correspondence will depend a lot more on the pattern of the significant differences, which does not depend on sample size directly. It could be the case that the estimate for a single small group with high variance may be difficult to balance with other estimates of smaller sampling variability, but this will depend on the values of the estimates, too.

\subsection{Tests and miscellaneous issues}\label{tests-and-miscellaneous-issues}

The function has several checks to ensure inputs are appropriate. The number of estimates has to be the same as the number of rows in the variance-covariance matrix. These checks will prevent the user from submitting incompatible estimates and variance-covariance matrices. Further, there is a check for symmetry and positive-definiteness of the variance-covariance matrix. If these checks fail, the function will return an error. If you suspect the error is because of floating-point issues, there is a tolerance that can be tweaked via the \texttt{tol} argument. We have also implemented a series of unit tests that ensure the function works as expected under a variety of scenarios.

There are two edge cases that may result in unexpected results. The first is when all estimates are statistically different from each other. In this case, the lower bound of acceptable intervals will be whatever the lower bound of \texttt{range\_levels} is. Making the confidence bounds increasingly smaller will increase the contrast of the non-overlapping nature of the intervals. Picking a level that makes the differences clear, but without making the intervals unnecessarily small would be a good fix. Likewise, if no estimates are statistically different from any others, the upper bound of the acceptable range will be the upper bound of \texttt{range\_levels}. Making the intervals increasingly larger will increase the contrast on the overlapping nature of all intervals. Again, using a value that identifies the overlap, but does not make the intervals unnecessarily large would be a good fix.

There is currently no mechanism to identify a priority in estimates whose differences are captured by the algorithm. One could imagine a situation where estimates of theoretical importance are given priority and the inferential confidence intervals are found such that those estimates are captured for certain and others are accommodated as is possible. One way of doing this would be to call \texttt{viztest()} on the important estimates first to find the range of acceptable levels. Then run \texttt{viztest()} on the full set of estimates and print the results using the levels identified in the initial run. This will identify the tests that are not appropriately captured by the inferential confidence level that works for the important estimates.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

As \citet{armstrong_2025} show, inferential confidence intervals can help solve the visual testing problem. By choosing the appropriate inferential confidence level, users can perform pairwise statistical tests accurately and reliably in most cases. The software described in this article allows users to calculate these inferential confidence levels in R for a wide class of objects using both normal-theory tests as well as tests on Bayesian posterior simulations. Users can interrogate these results with the package's \texttt{print()} method and plot the results either with the package's \texttt{plot()} method or via their graphing tool of choice.
